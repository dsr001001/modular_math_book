## 1. Global conventions fixed in this chapter

* **C1 (rings).** Rings are commutative with $1$, homomorphisms send $1$ to $1$, subrings contain $1$. The zero ring is
  allowed; fields and integral domains are nonzero. $R^\times$ is the unit group. (The paper's $\T$ is such a ring;
  matrix rings appear only as examples and are flagged as non-commutative.)
* **C2 (primes and $q$).** $p$, $q$, $\ell$ denote primes unless stated. In Sections 6 and 7 of the chapter $q=p^r$ is a
  prime power and $\F_q$ a finite field with $q$ elements. **In the paper $q$ is a prime** (a bad prime of $E$), so
  $\F_q=\Z/q\Z$ there and "primitive root mod $q$" applies; say this in the paperlink of Section 7. $\ell$ is the paper's
  coefficient prime, odd in all uses. Chapter 1's warning (its C4: field size versus bad prime) is repeated once.
* **C3 (valuations).** $v_p\colon\Q\to\Z\cup\{+\infty\}$ with $v_p(0)=+\infty$; $|x|_p=p^{-v_p(x)}$, $|0|_p=0$. For an ideal
  $r\Z$, $v_p(r\Z):=v_p(r)$. For $c\in\Z/\ell^m\Z$ put $v_\ell(c):=\min(v_\ell(\tilde c),m)$ for any lift $\tilde c\in\Z$ (well
  defined; so $v_\ell(0)=m$); then $v_\ell(c)\ge k$ iff $c\in\ell^k\Z/\ell^m\Z$, for $0\le k\le m$. With this convention the
  paper's $\min(v_\ell(n),v_\ell(q-1),v_\ell(c))$ is meaningful also when $c=0$, and when $v_\ell(q-1)=0$ (then $c=0$ in the
  zero group and the minimum is $0$).
* **C4 (symbols).** $\bigl(\frac ap\bigr)$ Legendre ($p$ odd prime), $\bigl(\frac an\bigr)$ Jacobi ($n$ odd positive), and the Kronecker
  symbol $\bigl(\frac an\bigr)$ for all $a\in\Z$, $n\in\Z$, with $\bigl(\frac a2\bigr)=0$ for $a$ even, $1$ for $a\equiv\pm1\pmod8$, $-1$ for
  $a\equiv\pm3\pmod8$; $\bigl(\frac a{-1}\bigr)=1$ for $a\ge0$ and $-1$ for $a<0$; $\bigl(\frac a1\bigr)=1$; $\bigl(\frac a0\bigr)=1$ if $a=\pm1$, else $0$.
* **C5 (Dirichlet characters and their names).** A Dirichlet character mod $n$ is a function $\chi\colon\Z\to\C$ coming
  from a homomorphism $(\Z/n)^\times\to\C^\times$, with $\chi(a)=0$ when $\gcd(a,n)>1$. Quadratic characters are named by
  a discriminant: $\chi_D(a)=\bigl(\frac Da\bigr)$ for $D\equiv0,1\pmod4$. So $\chi_{-3}=\bigl(\frac{-3}\cdot\bigr)$, $\chi_{-4}=\bigl(\frac{-4}\cdot\bigr)$. For
  an odd prime $q$, $q^*=(-1)^{(q-1)/2}q$ and, **as in the paper**, $\chi_q:=\chi_{q^*}$, "the quadratic character of
  conductor $q$" (so $\chi_3=\chi_{-3}$, $\chi_7=\chi_{-7}$, $\chi_{11}=\chi_{-11}$, $\chi_5=\chi_5$, $\chi_{13}=\chi_{13}$). **Warning:** the
  paper also writes $\chi_\ell$ for the $\ell$-adic cyclotomic character (Section 4, Lemmas lem:tamfactor, lem:old,
  lem:h0) and $\bar\chi_\ell$ for its reduction. In the chapter's own text write $\chi_{q^*}$ or $\bigl(\frac\cdot q\bigr)$; use
  the paper's $\chi_q$ only inside `paperlink` environments, and write $\omega_\ell\colon a\mapsto a\bmod\ell\in\F_\ell^\times$ for
  the Dirichlet character that corresponds to $\bar\chi_\ell$ (Chapters 5, 6 make the correspondence).
  These conventions agree with Chapter 5 (C3, C4) and Chapter 6 (4.2, 5.8).
* **C6 (roots of unity).** $\mu_n(K)=\{x\in K:x^n=1\}$; $\zeta_n=e^{2\pi i/n}\in\C$; $\zeta_3=(-1+\sqrt{-3})/2$ (never written $\omega$, which the book reserves for differentials and fundamental characters: Appendix B, B-C15(b)) with
  $\sqrt{-3}:=1+2\zeta_3=i\sqrt3$ (the sign fixed as in Chapter 6). Do not use $\pi$ for $1-\zeta_3$ in this chapter (that
  is Chapter 6's notation); $\pi$ is $3.14159\ldots$ here.
* **C7 (typography).** PLAN.md Section 7.2: no dash ligatures, ASCII source, labels `...:rings:...`.

## 2. Line-up of the core results, in teaching order

### Section 1. Rings, ideals, quotients, products

**1.1 `def:rings:ring`** (definition and proposition; P). A ring (C1), its unit group $R^\times$, zero divisors,
nilpotent elements, fields, integral domains, subrings, homomorphisms, kernel and image. Examples: $\Z$, $\Q$, $\R$,
$\C$, $\Z/n\Z$, $R[x]$, $R_1\times R_2$, the subring $\Z[i]\subset\C$, a commutative subring of $\End_\Z(A)$ for an abelian
group $A$ (the shape of the Hecke algebra $\T$). For every ring $R$ there is a unique homomorphism $\Z\to R$; its
kernel is $n\Z$ for a unique $n\ge0$, the characteristic of $R$. A domain has characteristic $0$ or a prime. A
finite domain is a field ($x\mapsto ax$ is injective, hence bijective). Reference: Dummit-Foote (DF) 7.1 to 7.3.
Paper: background ($\T$ is "the subring of $\End S_2(\Gamma_0(N))$ generated over $\Z$ by all $T_n$", Section 3).

**1.2 `def:rings:ideal`** (definition and proposition; P). An ideal $I\subset R$; the ideal $(a_1,\dots,a_n)$
generated by elements; sum $I+J$, product $IJ$, intersection $I\cap J$, with $IJ\subset I\cap J$; the annihilator
$\operatorname{Ann}_R(S)=\{r:rS=0\}$ of a subset of an $R$-module (definition only; modules are Chapter 3); $I$ and $J$ are
comaximal if $I+J=R$. The quotient ring $R/I$ and the projection $R\to R/I$; kernels of homomorphisms are exactly the
ideals. Every ideal of $\Z$ is $n\Z$ for a unique $n\ge0$ (division with remainder), and $m\Z+n\Z=\gcd(m,n)\Z$,
$m\Z\cap n\Z=\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)\Z$. Reference: DF 7.3, 7.4. Paper: Lemma lem:crit ($I=\ker\phi_f$, $J=\operatorname{Ann}_\T(f^\perp)$,
$I+J$), Lemma lem:ddt417 ($\wp=\ker\pi$, $I=\operatorname{Ann}_\T(\wp)$, the congruence ideal $\eta=\pi(I)$).

**1.3 `thm:rings:isomorphism`** (theorem; P). (i) A homomorphism $\phi\colon R\to S$ induces an isomorphism
$R/\ker\phi\simeq\phi(R)$. (ii) (Correspondence.) For an ideal $I$, $J\mapsto J/I$ is an inclusion-preserving bijection from
ideals of $R$ containing $I$ to ideals of $R/I$, with $(R/I)/(J/I)\simeq R/J$; prime and maximal ideals correspond
(after 2.1). (iii) If $\phi\colon R\to S$ is **surjective** with kernel $I$ and $J\subset R$ is an ideal, then $\phi(J)$ is an
ideal of $S$ and $\phi$ induces $R/(I+J)\simeq S/\phi(J)$. (iv) In particular every quotient ring of $\Z$ is $\Z/r\Z$ for a
unique $r\ge0$, cyclic as an additive group. Reference: DF 7.3. Paper: Lemma lem:crit ("$\T/(I+J)\simeq\Z/\phi_f(J)$ is cyclic",
with $\phi_f\colon\T\to\Z$ surjective because $\phi_f(T_1)=1$; "equivalently, $r$ generates the congruence ideal
$\phi_f(J)$"), Lemma lem:hecke ($r_E\Z_\ell$ is the congruence ideal).

**1.4 `prop:rings:two-ideals`** (proposition; P). Let $I,J$ be ideals of a ring $R$. The sequence of additive groups
(indeed of $R$-modules)
$$0\to R/(I\cap J)\xrightarrow{\ x\mapsto(x,x)\ }R/I\times R/J\xrightarrow{\ (y,z)\mapsto y-z\ }R/(I+J)\to0$$
is exact. Consequently: (i) if $I\cap J=0$, then $R$ embeds in $R/I\times R/J$ with cokernel $R/(I+J)$; (ii) (Chinese remainder
theorem for two ideals) if $I+J=R$ then $I\cap J=IJ$ and $R/IJ\simeq R/I\times R/J$ as rings; (iii) (general CRT) if
$I_1,\dots,I_k$ are pairwise comaximal then $R/(I_1\cdots I_k)\simeq\prod_iR/I_i$. Reference: DF 7.6. Paper: Lemma lem:crit
("Dualising the inclusion $\T\subset\T/I\times\T/J$, whose cokernel is $\T/(I+J)$", with $I\cap J=0$); the module analogue
$L[\wp]\oplus L[I]\subset L$ in Lemma lem:ddt417 is Chapter 3; Chapter 6, item 2.4 (CRT for Dedekind domains) cites (iii).

**1.5 `prop:rings:idempotents`** (definition and proposition; P). An idempotent is $e\in R$ with $e^2=e$; then $1-e$ is
idempotent and $R\to Re\times R(1-e)$, $x\mapsto(xe,x(1-e))$, is a ring isomorphism ($Re$ is a ring with identity $e$).
$R$ is isomorphic to a product of two nonzero rings iff it has an idempotent $e\ne0,1$. The ideals of $R_1\times R_2$ are
the $I_1\times I_2$; its prime ideals are $P_1\times R_2$ and $R_1\times P_2$. Example: $\Q[x]/(x^2-x)\simeq\Q\times\Q$; more generally
$K[x]/(f)\simeq\prod_iK[x]/(f_i)$ for $f=\prod f_i$ with pairwise coprime $f_i$ (by 1.4(iii)). Reference: DF 7.6;
Atiyah-Macdonald Chapter 1 (exercises). Paper: Lemma lem:crit ("$\T\otimes\Q=\Q\times\T'$", $I=\T\cap(0\times\T')$,
$J=\T\cap(\Q\times0)$), Theorem thm:quant Step 1 (the idempotent of $\T_\ell$ projecting to the $\mathfrak m$-component; the
general statement for finite $\Z_\ell$-algebras is Chapter 3).

**1.6 `prop:rings:freshman`** (proposition; P). If $R$ has prime characteristic $p$ then $\binom pi\equiv0\pmod p$ for
$0<i<p$, so $(x+y)^p=x^p+y^p$ and $F\colon x\mapsto x^p$ is a ring endomorphism of $R$ (the absolute Frobenius); also
$(x+y)^{p^k}=x^{p^k}+y^{p^k}$. $F$ is injective if $R$ is a domain and bijective if $R$ is a finite domain (a finite
field). Corollary (Fermat): $a^p\equiv a\pmod p$ for all $a\in\Z$. Reference: DF 13.5. Paper: background (finite
fields, Section 6; the Frobenius of Chapters 5 and 6).

### Section 2. Prime and maximal ideals, domains, fractions, $\Z_{(p)}$

**2.1 `thm:rings:prime-maximal`** (definition and theorem; P). An ideal $P\ne R$ is prime if $ab\in P$ implies $a\in P$
or $b\in P$; an ideal $M\ne R$ is maximal if no ideal lies strictly between $M$ and $R$. (i) $R/P$ is a domain iff $P$ is
prime; $R/M$ is a field iff $M$ is maximal; maximal ideals are prime. (ii) The preimage of a prime ideal under a
homomorphism is prime; the kernel of a homomorphism $R\to K$, $K$ a field, is prime, and maximal if the map is
surjective (or if its image is finite, by 1.1). (iii) If $R/P$ is finite then $P$ is prime iff maximal. (iv) The prime
ideals of $\Z$ are $0$ and $p\Z$; the maximal ones are the $p\Z$. (v) Every nonzero ring has a maximal ideal (Zorn's
lemma; stated, with the elementary proof for Noetherian rings, Chapter 3); every non-unit lies in a maximal ideal.
*P except (v), R.* Reference: DF 7.4. Paper: Lemma lem:crit ("$I+J$ lies in the kernel of $\T\to\mathcal O/\lambda$, a maximal
ideal of residue characteristic $\ell$"), Lemma lem:hecke and Theorem thm:quant ($\mathfrak m$, the maximal ideal of $\T\otimes\Z_\ell$
containing $\ell$ and $\ker\phi_f$).

**2.2 `prop:rings:residue-char`** (proposition; P). Let $\mathfrak m$ be a maximal ideal of a ring $R$ such that $R/\mathfrak m$ has
characteristic $\ell>0$ ("residue characteristic $\ell$"). Then $\ell\cdot1\in\mathfrak m$. If $\mathfrak a\subset\mathfrak m$ is an ideal with
$R/\mathfrak a$ finite, then $\ell\mid\#(R/\mathfrak a)$ (because $R/\mathfrak a$ surjects onto the field $R/\mathfrak m$, an $\F_\ell$-vector
space of dimension $\ge1$, and additive orders multiply along the quotient). In particular a ring homomorphism
$\Z/r\Z\to k$ into a field of characteristic $\ell$ exists iff $\ell\mid r$ (for $r\ge1$). Reference: elementary. Paper:
Lemma lem:crit, last step ("and $\ell$ divides $\#\T/(I+J)=r$"); Lemma lem:old ("after enlarging $\mathcal O$ and $\lambda$"); Chapter
6, item 7.3, cites this.

**2.3 `thm:rings:fractions`** (definition and theorem; P). For a domain $R$ the field of fractions $\operatorname{Frac}(R)$
(pairs $a/b$, $b\ne0$, modulo $ad=bc$) is a field containing $R$, with the universal property: every injective
homomorphism $R\to K$ into a field extends uniquely to $\operatorname{Frac}(R)\to K$. Examples: $\operatorname{Frac}(\Z)=\Q$,
$\operatorname{Frac}(F[x])=F(x)$, $\operatorname{Frac}(\Z[i])=\Q(i)$, $\operatorname{Frac}(\Z[\zeta_3])=\Q(\zeta_3)=\Q(\sqrt{-3})$. Reference: DF 7.5.
Paper: background ($\Q$; number fields in Chapter 6).

**2.4 `prop:rings:zp-local`** (definition and proposition; P). For a prime $p$ let
$\Z_{(p)}=\{a/b\in\Q:p\nmid b\}$. (i) $\Z_{(p)}$ is a subring of $\Q$ with $\Z_{(p)}^\times=\{a/b:p\nmid ab\}$; an integer prime to $p$
is a unit of $\Z_{(p)}$. (ii) Its nonzero ideals are the $p^k\Z_{(p)}$, $k\ge0$; it has exactly one maximal ideal
$p\Z_{(p)}$ (it is a local ring; the first example of a discrete valuation ring, Chapter 3). (iii) The reduction map
$\Z_{(p)}\to\F_p$, $a/b\mapsto\bar a\,\bar b^{-1}$, is a well-defined surjective ring homomorphism with kernel $p\Z_{(p)}$; it
maps $\Z_{(p)}^\times$ onto $\F_p^\times$. (iv) $\Z_{(p)}=\{x\in\Q:v_p(x)\ge0\}$ and $\Z_{(p)}^\times=\{x:v_p(x)=0\}$ (11.1). (v)
$\Z=\bigcap_p\Z_{(p)}$. Reference: Atiyah-Macdonald Chapter 3 (localisation); Serre, Course in Arithmetic, Chapter II.
Paper: Lemma lem:fpr ("$L_p(A_f,1)^{-1}$ is a nonzero rational number which is an $\ell$-adic integer"), Definition def:terms
($q$ is an $\ell$-adic unit), Lemma lem:tamfactor (the class in $\F_q^\times$ of the unit part $u$ of the Tate parameter is its
reduction; Chapter 7 replaces $\Z_{(q)}$ by $\Z_q$). Forward: Chapter 3 (localisation, DVRs, $\Z_\ell$), Chapter 7 (completion),
Chapter 16 ("an integer prime to $\ell$ is a unit of $\Z_\ell$").

### Section 3. Euclidean domains, PIDs, UFDs; $\Z[i]$ and $\Z[\zeta_3]$

**3.1 `def:rings:factorisation`** (definitions and proposition; P). In a domain $R$: divisibility, units, associates,
irreducible elements, prime elements; a prime element is irreducible; $(a)$ is a nonzero prime ideal iff $a$ is a
prime element. $R$ is a *Euclidean domain* if there is $N\colon R\setminus\{0\}\to\Z_{\ge0}$ such that for $a$, $b\ne0$ there are
$\kappa,\rho$ with $a=\kappa b+\rho$ and $\rho=0$ or $N(\rho)<N(b)$; a *principal ideal domain* (PID) if every ideal is
principal; a *unique factorisation domain* (UFD) if every nonzero non-unit is a product of irreducibles, uniquely up
to order and units. A greatest common divisor. Reference: DF 8.1 to 8.3. Paper: background.

**3.2 `thm:rings:euclid-pid-ufd`** (theorem; P). (i) A Euclidean domain is a PID. (ii) A PID is Noetherian (ascending
chains of ideals stabilise) and a UFD; in a PID irreducible elements are prime, nonzero prime ideals are maximal, and
$\gcd(a,b)$ exists with Bezout's identity $(a,b)=(\gcd(a,b))$. (iii) In a UFD irreducible elements are prime and gcds
exist. (iv) Examples: $\Z$ (absolute value), $F[x]$ for a field $F$ (degree; 4.1); $\Z[x]$ and $F[x,y]$ are UFDs (4.3) but not
PIDs ($(2,x)$, $(x,y)$). Reference: DF 8.1 to 8.3. Paper: background ($\Z$, $\F_\ell[x]$; $\Z_\ell$ is a PID, Chapter 3).

**3.3 `prop:rings:integers`** (proposition; P). In $\Z$: Bezout, Euclid's lemma ($p\mid ab\Rightarrow p\mid a$ or $p\mid b$), the
fundamental theorem of arithmetic $n=\pm\prod_pp^{v_p(n)}$, and $\gcd(m,n)=\prod p^{\min(v_p(m),v_p(n))}$,
$\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)=\prod p^{\max(v_p(m),v_p(n))}$; the $p$-part of $n\ne0$ is $p^{v_p(n)}$. Reference: DF 8.1, 8.3. Paper:
background ($v_\ell(\deg\varphi_E)$, "the $\ell$-part of $r$", Agashe-Ribet-Stein's $v_p(\deg\varphi_E)=v_p(r_E)$).

**3.4 `thm:rings:gauss-eisenstein-integers`** (theorem; P). In $\Z[i]=\{a+bi\}$ and
$\Z[\zeta_3]=\{a+b\zeta_3\}$ (subrings of $\C$; $\zeta_3^2=-1-\zeta_3$) put $N(z)=z\bar z=|z|^2$, so $N(a+bi)=a^2+b^2$ and
$N(a+b\zeta_3)=a^2-ab+b^2$, nonnegative integers, multiplicative. (i) Both rings are Euclidean for $N$: for $\alpha$, $\beta\ne0$
there is $\kappa$ with $N(\alpha-\kappa\beta)\le\frac12N(\beta)$ in $\Z[i]$ and $\le\frac13N(\beta)$ in $\Z[\zeta_3]$ (round $\alpha/\beta\in\Q(i)$, resp.
$\Q(\zeta_3)$, to a nearest lattice point; the worst cases are the centre of a square of side $1$, resp. the centroid of an
equilateral triangle of side $1$). Hence both are PIDs and UFDs. (ii) $z$ is a unit iff $N(z)=1$; $\Z[i]^\times=\{\pm1,\pm i\}=\mu_4$
and $\Z[\zeta_3]^\times=\{\pm1,\pm\zeta_3,\pm\zeta_3^2\}=\mu_6$. (iii) An element of prime norm is irreducible, hence prime.
(iv) Examples: $2=-i(1+i)^2$, $5=(2+i)(2-i)$, $7=(3+\zeta_3)(3+\zeta_3^2)$, $13=(4+\zeta_3)(4+\zeta_3^2)$, $3=-\zeta_3^2(1-\zeta_3)^2$, and a worked
Euclidean algorithm in $\Z[i]$: $11+3i=(1-i)(1+8i)+(2-4i)$, $1+8i=(-2+i)(2-4i)+(1-2i)$, $2-4i=2(1-2i)$, so
$\gcd(11+3i,1+8i)=1-2i$, of norm $5$ (checked). The split: the
list of all primes of $\Z[i]$ and $\Z[\zeta_3]$ ($p$ splits iff $p\equiv1\pmod4$, resp. $\pmod3$), the ramified prime $1-\zeta_3$, the
DVR $\Z_3[\zeta_3]$ and $\Z_\ell[\zeta_3]$ are Chapter 6 (items 4.5 to 4.9), which cites (i) and (ii) from here. Reference: DF 8.1
($\Z[i]$); Ireland-Rosen, Chapters 1 and 9. Paper: background for Lemma lem:IV ($\Z_3[\zeta_3]$), Lemma lem:euler ($\Z_\ell[\zeta_3]$,
$\Z_\ell[i]$), and the table of Section 1 ($\chi_{-3}$, $\chi_{-4}$ are the characters of $\Q(\zeta_3)$, $\Q(i)$).

**3.5 `ex:rings:non-ufd`** (example; P). In $\Z[\sqrt{-5}]$, $6=2\cdot3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ are two factorisations into
irreducibles (norms $4$, $9$, $6$, $6$; there is no element of norm $2$ or $3$), none of the four factors associate; so
$\Z[\sqrt{-5}]$ is neither a UFD nor a PID ($(2,1+\sqrt{-5})$ is not principal). The ideal-theoretic repair is Chapter 6
(items 2.2, 2.6). Similarly $4=2\cdot2=(1+\sqrt{-3})(1-\sqrt{-3})$ in $\Z[\sqrt{-3}]\subsetneq\Z[\zeta_3]$ (Chapter 6, Exercise 2). Paper:
background (why the rings $\mathcal O$ of Lemmas lem:crit and lem:old are handled through ideals).

### Section 4. Polynomial rings and irreducibility

**4.1 `prop:rings:poly-division`** (proposition; P). $R[x]$; degree; over a domain $\deg(fg)=\deg f+\deg g$, $R[x]$ is a
domain and $R[x]^\times=R^\times$. (i) Division with remainder by a **monic** $g$ over any ring: $f=hg+r$, $\deg r<\deg g$,
uniquely. (ii) For a field $F$, $F[x]$ is Euclidean (degree), hence a PID and a UFD; its maximal ideals are the $(f)$
with $f$ irreducible. (iii) Factor theorem: $f(a)=0$ iff $(x-a)\mid f$. (iv) A nonzero polynomial of degree $d$ over a
domain has at most $d$ roots in the domain. (v) Polynomials versus functions: $x^p-x\in\F_p[x]$ is nonzero but vanishes on
$\F_p$; over an infinite domain a polynomial is determined by its values. Reference: DF 9.1 to 9.5. Paper: background
((iv) is the input of Chapter 1, item 4.2: finite subgroups of $K^\times$ are cyclic).

**4.2 `prop:rings:derivative`** (proposition; P). The formal derivative $f'$, with $(fg)'=f'g+fg'$. For $f\in F[x]$ and an
extension field $K\supseteq F$: $a\in K$ is a multiple root of $f$ iff $f(a)=f'(a)=0$; $f$ has no repeated root in any
extension iff $\gcd(f,f')=1$ in $F[x]$ (the gcd does not change under extension of the field). Consequences: in
characteristic $p$, $x^{p^r}-x$ has derivative $-1$, so it has $p^r$ distinct roots in any field where it splits;
$x^n-1$ is separable over $F$ iff $\operatorname{char}F\nmid n$. Reference: DF 13.5. Paper: background (finite fields, 6.2;
roots of unity, 8.1; separability in Chapter 5).

**4.3 `thm:rings:gauss-lemma`** (theorem, Gauss's lemma; P for (i), (ii), (iv); S for (iii)). Let $R$ be a UFD with
fraction field $K$. The content $c(f)$ of $0\ne f\in R[x]$ is a gcd of its coefficients (defined up to units); $f$ is
primitive if $c(f)$ is a unit. (i) $c(fg)=c(f)c(g)$ up to units; a product of primitive polynomials is primitive. (ii) If
$f\in R[x]$ factors as $f=gh$ in $K[x]$, then $f=g_1h_1$ in $R[x]$ with $g_1\in K^\times g$, $h_1\in K^\times h$. Hence a
primitive $f$ of positive degree is irreducible in $R[x]$ iff it is irreducible in $K[x]$. (iii) $R[x]$ is a UFD (so
$\Z[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ and $F[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ are UFDs). (iv) Corollaries: a monic $f\in\Z[x]$ that factors into monic
polynomials in $\Q[x]$ has those factors in $\Z[x]$; the rational root test (if $a/b$ in lowest terms is a root of
$a_nx^n+\dots+a_0\in\Z[x]$ then $b\mid a_n$ and $a\mid a_0$); a rational root of a monic integer polynomial is an integer.
Reference: DF 9.3; Lang, Algebra IV.2. Paper: background (Chapter 6, item 1.2: the minimal polynomial of an algebraic
integer lies in $\Z[x]$; Chapter 5: $\Phi_n\in\Z[x]$ irreducible).

**4.4 `thm:rings:eisenstein`** (theorem, Eisenstein's criterion; P). Let $R$ be a domain, $P$ a prime ideal of $R$, and
$f=a_nx^n+\dots+a_0\in R[x]$, $n\ge1$, with $a_n\notin P$, $a_i\in P$ for $i<n$, and $a_0\notin P^2$. Then $f$ is not a product of
two polynomials of positive degree in $R[x]$; if $R$ is a UFD and $f$ is primitive, $f$ is irreducible in $R[x]$ and in
$K[x]$ (4.3). Variants: apply it to $f(x+a)$. Examples: $x^n-p$; $\Phi_p(x+1)=\bigl((x+1)^p-1\bigr)/x$; $\Phi_{p^k}(x+1)$ (8.2);
$\Phi_3(x+1)=x^2+3x+3$. Reference: DF 9.4. Paper: background (Chapter 5: irreducibility of $\Phi_p$; Chapter 6, items 4.7
and 5.1: $\Z_3[\zeta_3]$ a DVR, $\Z[\zeta_{\ell^a}]$ is $\ell$-maximal).

**4.5 `prop:rings:reduction-mod-p`** (proposition; P). Let $f\in\Z[x]$ of degree $n$ and $p$ a prime not dividing the leading
coefficient. If $f=gh$ in $\Z[x]$ then $\bar f=\bar g\bar h$ in $\F_p[x]$ with $\deg\bar g=\deg g$, $\deg\bar h=\deg h$. Hence: (i) if
$f$ is primitive and $\bar f$ is irreducible in $\F_p[x]$, $f$ is irreducible in $\Q[x]$; (ii) more generally the degrees of
any factorisation of $f$ over $\Q$ are sums of degrees of the irreducible factors of $\bar f$, for every such $p$, and two
primes with incompatible patterns prove irreducibility. Warning: $x^4+1$ is irreducible over $\Q$ (Eisenstein after
$x\mapsto x+1$) but reducible modulo every prime (checked for $p<500$; proof: $\F_{p^2}$ contains a primitive $8$-th root of unity,
so every root generates a field of degree at most $2$). Example: the $3$-division polynomial of 11a1,
$\psi_3=3x^4-4x^3-60x^2-237x-21$, is irreducible modulo $5$, hence over $\Q$. Reference: DF 9.4. Paper: Proposition prop:ns
("otherwise it [the $3$-division polynomial] is irreducible": a test for a given curve), Section sec:data ("checked against
the factorisation of the $3$-division polynomial"); the Galois-theoretic meaning of the pattern is Chapter 5.

**4.6 `thm:rings:adjoin-root`** (theorem, Kronecker's construction; P). Let $F$ be a field and $f\in F[x]$ irreducible of degree
$d$. (i) $F[x]/(f)$ is a field containing $F$, of dimension $d$ over $F$ with basis $1,\bar x,\dots,\bar x^{d-1}$, in which
$\bar x$ is a root of $f$. (ii) If $\alpha$ is a root of $f$ in a field $K\supseteq F$, evaluation induces an isomorphism
$F[x]/(f)\simeq F[\alpha]$, and $F[\alpha]$ is a field. (iii) For every nonconstant $g\in F[x]$ there is a field $K\supseteq F$ of
finite dimension over $F$ in which $g$ is a product of linear factors (iterate (i)). Examples: $\F_3[x]/(x^2+1)=\F_9$;
$\F_q[x]/(x^2-\varepsilon)$ for a non-square $\varepsilon$ (Chapter 1, item 5.8). Splitting fields and their uniqueness in general are
Chapter 5. Reference: DF 13.1, 13.4. Paper: background ($\F_9$ in Proposition prop:ns: "$\bar\rho|\otimes\F_9=\psi\oplus\psi^{-1}$").

### Section 5. $\Z/n\Z$, the Chinese remainder theorem, units and primitive roots

**5.1 `prop:rings:zn`** (proposition; P). (i) $a\bmod n$ is a unit of $\Z/n\Z$ iff $\gcd(a,n)=1$ (Bezout); $\Z/n\Z$ is a field
iff it is a domain iff $n$ is prime; $\F_p:=\Z/p\Z$. (ii) $\#(\Z/n\Z)^\times=\varphi(n)$; Euler's theorem $a^{\varphi(n)}\equiv1\pmod n$ for
$\gcd(a,n)=1$ (Lagrange, Chapter 1); $\varphi(p^k)=p^{k-1}(p-1)$; $\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d)=n$. (iii) $(\Z/n\Z)^\times=\Aut(\Z/n)$, acting by
multiplication (Chapter 1, item 1.3(iii)). Note $\Z/p^k\Z$ ($k\ge2$) is not a field and is not $\F_{p^k}$. Reference: DF 0.3,
7.3. Paper: background ($\F_q=\Z/q\Z$ for the paper's prime $q$; the groups on which Dirichlet characters live).

**5.2 `thm:rings:crt`** (theorem, Chinese remainder theorem; P). For $n=\prod_ip_i^{k_i}$ the reduction maps give a ring
isomorphism $\Z/n\Z\simeq\prod_i\Z/p_i^{k_i}\Z$; more generally $\Z/mn\Z\simeq\Z/m\Z\times\Z/n\Z$ iff $\gcd(m,n)=1$. Hence
$(\Z/n\Z)^\times\simeq\prod_i(\Z/p_i^{k_i}\Z)^\times$ and $\varphi$ is multiplicative. Proof from 1.4(iii); the group version is
Chapter 1, item 2.2. Reference: DF 7.6. Paper: background (Dirichlet characters split over prime powers, 10.3(iv);
the conductor $\prod_{q\in S}q$ of $\chi_S$; the decomposition of a cyclic group into $\ell$-parts in Lemma lem:tamfactor).

**5.3 `thm:rings:primitive-roots`** (theorem; P). (i) $(\Z/p\Z)^\times=\F_p^\times$ is cyclic of order $p-1$ (Chapter 1, item 4.2);
a generator is a primitive root mod $p$. (ii) For $p$ odd and $k\ge1$, $(\Z/p^k\Z)^\times$ is cyclic of order $p^{k-1}(p-1)$: the
class of $1+p$ has order $p^{k-1}$ (5.4(iii)), and if $g$ is a primitive root mod $p$ with $g^{p-1}\not\equiv1\pmod{p^2}$ (true for $g$
or $g+p$) then $g$ is a primitive root mod $p^k$ for every $k$. (iii) $(\Z/2\Z)^\times$ and $(\Z/4\Z)^\times$ are cyclic; for $k\ge3$,
$(\Z/2^k\Z)^\times=\{\pm1\}\times\langle5\rangle\simeq\Z/2\times\Z/2^{k-2}$ is not cyclic ($5$ has order $2^{k-2}$, and $-1\notin\langle5\rangle$). (iv)
$(\Z/n\Z)^\times$ is cyclic iff $n\in\{1,2,4,p^k,2p^k\}$ with $p$ an odd prime (checked for $n<400$). Reference: Ireland-Rosen,
Chapter 4; Serre, Course in Arithmetic, Chapter II (in $p$-adic form). Paper: background (Chapter 7: the structure of
$\Z_p^\times$; Dirichlet characters mod $p^k$; Chapter 5's $\chi_\ell(G_\Q)=\Z_\ell^\times$ maps onto the cyclic $(\Z/\ell^k)^\times$).

**5.4 `lem:rings:lte`** (lemma, lifting the exponent; P). (i) Let $\ell$ be an odd prime and $a,b\in\Z$ with $\ell\mid a-b$ and
$\ell\nmid ab$. Then $v_\ell(a^n-b^n)=v_\ell(a-b)+v_\ell(n)$ for every $n\ge1$. (ii) For $a$ odd and $n$ even,
$v_2(a^n-1)=v_2(a-1)+v_2(a+1)+v_2(n)-1$. (iii) Consequently, for $\ell$ odd, $(1+\ell)^{\ell^{k-1}}\equiv1+\ell^k\pmod{\ell^{k+1}}$ and $1+\ell$
has order $\ell^{k-1}$ in $(\Z/\ell^k\Z)^\times$. (iv) For primes $q\ne\ell$, $\ell$ odd, and $d$ the order of $q$ mod $\ell$: $v_\ell(q^f-1)=0$ if
$d\nmid f$, and $v_\ell(q^f-1)=v_\ell(q^d-1)+v_\ell(f/d)$ if $d\mid f$. (v) $v_\ell(q^2-1)=v_\ell(q-1)+v_\ell(q+1)$, and for odd $\ell\ne q$ at most one
of the two terms is positive. Reference: elementary (Ireland-Rosen, Chapter 4, exercises). Paper: the $\mathrm{I}_n^*$ entry
$v_\ell(q^2-1)$ of the table; background for Chapter 7 ($v_\ell(\#\F_{q^f}^\times)$, the $\ell$-power roots of unity in unramified
extensions) and Chapter 5 (smallest $f$ with $\mu_{\ell^k}\subset\F_{q^f}$).

**5.5 `cor:rings:squares-one`** (corollary; P). Let $\ell$ be an odd prime and $k\ge1$. The solutions of $a^2\equiv1\pmod{\ell^k}$
are $a\equiv\pm1$ (as $(\Z/\ell^k)^\times$ is cyclic of even order). Hence $a^2\equiv1\pmod{\ell^k}$ for **every** $a$ prime to $\ell$
iff $\varphi(\ell^k)=2$ iff $\ell^k=3$. In particular, if a homomorphism $\chi$ from a group onto $\Z_\ell^\times$ (or onto
$(\Z/\ell^k)^\times$) satisfies $\chi^2\equiv1\pmod{\ell^k}$, then $\ell^k=3$. Example: $2^2=4\not\equiv1\pmod9$. Reference: elementary.
Paper: Lemma lem:h0 in the corrected form of important_notes.md point 5 ("comparing determinants it forces
$\bar\chi_\ell^2=1$, so $\ell=3$"; and $h_3(E)\le1$ because $4\not\equiv1\bmod9$); used by Chapter 5, item galois:twist (c).

### Section 6. Finite fields

**6.1 `prop:rings:finite-field-basic`** (proposition; P). Let $F$ be a finite field. (i) $\operatorname{char}F=p$ is prime,
$F\supseteq\F_p$ (the image of $\Z$), and $\#F=p^r$ with $r=\dim_{\F_p}F$. (ii) $F^\times$ is cyclic of order $p^r-1$ (Chapter 1,
item 4.2, which rests on 4.1(iv)). (iii) Every $a\in F$ satisfies $a^{p^r}=a$, and $x^{p^r}-x=\prod_{a\in F}(x-a)$ in $F[x]$.
(iv) $F=\F_p(\alpha)=\F_p[\alpha]$ for any generator $\alpha$ of $F^\times$. Reference: DF 14.3; Serre, Course in Arithmetic, I.1.
Paper: Lemma lem:tamfactor ($\F_q^\times$ cyclic), background.

**6.2 `thm:rings:finite-field-exists`** (theorem, existence and uniqueness; P). Let $q=p^r$. (i) There is a field with $q$
elements: in a field $K\supseteq\F_p$ where $x^q-x$ splits (4.6(iii)) its roots are $q$ distinct elements (4.2) forming a subfield
(closed under sums by 1.6, under products and inverses clearly). (ii) Any two fields $K$, $K'$ with $q$ elements are
isomorphic: write $K=\F_p[\alpha]$ (6.1(iv)) and let $f$ be the monic irreducible polynomial of $\alpha$ over $\F_p$ (the monic generator
of the kernel of $\F_p[x]\to K$); $f$ divides $x^q-x$, which is $\prod_{b\in K'}(x-b)$, so $f$ has a root $\beta\in K'$, and
$K\simeq\F_p[x]/(f)\simeq\F_p[\beta]=K'$ by 4.6(ii) and counting. Write $\F_q$. (iii) $\F_q\simeq\F_p[x]/(f)$ for every irreducible
$f\in\F_p[x]$ of degree $r$. (iv) The isomorphism in (ii) is not unique (compose with powers of the Frobenius 6.3); inside a
fixed algebraic closure the subfield with $q$ elements is unique (Chapter 5). Reference: DF 14.3; Lang, Algebra V.5;
Serre, Course in Arithmetic, I.1. Paper: background ($\F_9=\F_3[i]$ in Proposition prop:ns; $\F_{q^2}$ of Chapter 1, item 5.8;
residue fields in Chapters 6 and 7).

**6.3 `prop:rings:frobenius`** (proposition; P). Let $q=p^r$. (i) $\Frob_p\colon x\mapsto x^p$ is a field automorphism of $\F_q$ (1.6),
its fixed points are exactly $\F_p$ (the $p$ roots of $x^p-x$), and $\Frob_p$ has order exactly $r$ (if $\Frob_p^s=1$ with $s<r$, all $q$
elements would be roots of $x^{p^s}-x$). (ii) For $d\mid r$, $\{x\in\F_q:x^{p^d}=x\}$ is a subfield with $p^d$ elements and the only one;
$\F_q$ contains a subfield with $p^d$ elements iff $d\mid r$ ($\F_{p^d}^\times\subseteq\F_q^\times$ forces $p^d-1\mid p^r-1$, i.e. $d\mid r$).
(iii) The fixed field of $\Frob_p^s$ is $\F_{p^{\gcd(s,r)}}$. (iv) (Remark with forward pointer, not a numbered result.) Every
automorphism of $\F_q$ is a power of $\Frob_p$, and $\Gal(\F_{q^n}/\F_q)$ is cyclic of order $n$ generated by $x\mapsto x^q$: Chapter 5,
item galois:finite-field-galois. Reference: DF 14.3; Lang, Algebra V.5. Paper: background (the arithmetic Frobenius of
Chapters 5 and 6 induces $x\mapsto x^q$ on residue fields; the "$\Frob$" of Lemmas lem:euler and lem:tamfactor).

**6.4 `prop:rings:irreducible-count`** (proposition; P). In $\F_p[x]$ (more generally $\F_q[x]$),
$x^{p^r}-x=\prod f$ over the monic irreducible $f$ with $\deg f\mid r$, each once. Hence $p^r=\sum_{d\mid r}dN_d$, where $N_d$ is the
number of monic irreducibles of degree $d$, and $N_r=\frac1r\sum_{d\mid r}\mu(d)p^{r/d}>0$ (Moebius inversion; positivity gives a
second existence proof). Examples: over $\F_2$, $N_1,\dots,N_4=2,1,2,3$; over $\F_3$, $N_2=3$ ($x^2+1$, $x^2+x+2$, $x^2+2x+2$).
Proof uses 6.1, 6.3(ii) and 4.2. Reference: Ireland-Rosen, Chapter 7; Lang, Algebra V.5. Paper: background.

**6.5 `prop:rings:mu-in-fq`** (proposition; P). Let $q=p^r$ and $n\ge1$. (i) $\mu_n(\F_q)$ is cyclic of order $\gcd(n,q-1)$; it has $n$
elements iff $n\mid q-1$. (ii) If $p\nmid n$, the least $f\ge1$ with $\mu_n\subset\F_{q^f}$ (i.e. with $n$ distinct $n$-th roots of unity) is the
order of $q$ in $(\Z/n)^\times$. (iii) In characteristic $p$, $x^{p^km}-1=(x^m-1)^{p^k}$: there are no nontrivial $p$-power roots of
unity. (iv) For $q$ odd: $-1$ is a square in $\F_q$ iff $q\equiv1\pmod4$; if moreover $p\ne3$, $-3$ is a square in $\F_q$ iff
$\mu_3\subset\F_q$ iff $q\equiv1\pmod3$ (because $(1+2\zeta_3)^2=-3$ for a primitive cube root of unity $\zeta_3$, and conversely
$\zeta_3=(-1+s)/2$ if $s^2=-3$). (v) Let $K$ be any field with $\operatorname{char}K\ne3$ containing $\mu_3$, and $m$ an integer prime to $3$; then
$\zeta\mapsto\zeta^m$ is the identity of $\mu_3$ if $m\equiv1\pmod3$ and inversion if $m\equiv2\pmod3$; likewise on $\mu_4$
($\operatorname{char}K\ne2$, $m$ odd) with $m\bmod4$. By 10.5 this says $\zeta^m=\zeta^{\chi_{-3}(m)}$ on $\mu_3$ and
$\zeta^m=\zeta^{\chi_{-4}(m)}$ on $\mu_4$. Reference: DF 14.3; Ireland-Rosen, Chapter 7. Paper: Lemma lem:euler ("Frobenius
centralises $\sigma$ if $q\equiv1\pmod3$ and inverts it if $q\equiv2$, so it acts on the line by $\chi_{-3}(q)$"; the same with $\mu_4$
and $\chi_{-4}(q)$: the arithmetic input, with the Galois-theoretic input in Chapter 6, items 3.11, 3.12); Chapter 5 (Kummer
theory needs $\mu_{\ell^k}\subset\F_q$ iff $\ell^k\mid q-1$); Chapter 7.

### Section 7. Powers in $\F_q^\times$

**7.1 `thm:rings:power-index`** (theorem; P). Let $q$ be a prime power, $m\ge1$ and $d=\gcd(m,q-1)$. (i) The $m$-th power map
on $\F_q^\times$ has kernel $\mu_d(\F_q)$, of order $d$, and image $(\F_q^\times)^m=(\F_q^\times)^d$, the unique subgroup of index $d$.
(ii) (Generalised Euler criterion.) $u\in\F_q^\times$ is an $m$-th power iff $u^{(q-1)/d}=1$. (iii) For a prime $\ell$ and $k\ge0$:
$$[\F_q^\times:(\F_q^\times)^{\ell^k}]=\ell^{\min(k,v_\ell(q-1))}.$$
In particular, if $q\not\equiv1\pmod\ell$ the $\ell$-th power map is bijective and every element is an $\ell^k$-th power; if
$q\equiv1\pmod\ell$, exactly a fraction $1/\ell$ of $\F_q^\times$ are $\ell$-th powers. (iv) ($m=2$, $q$ odd.) The squares form the
subgroup of index $2$; $u^{(q-1)/2}=\pm1$, with $+1$ iff $u$ is a square. Proof: Chapter 1, item 1.3(ii), applied to the
cyclic group $\F_q^\times$ (6.1). (Checked numerically for all primes $q<400$, $\ell\le13$, $k\le4$, and for $q=4,8,9,16,25,27,49,64,81$.)
Reference: Ireland-Rosen, Chapters 4 and 7; Serre, Course in Arithmetic, I.3. Paper: Lemma lem:tamfactor ("$u$ is an
$\ell^k$-th power in $\F_q^\times$, for $k\le v_\ell(q-1)$"), Section sec:tam, Remark rem:ko(1) ("$q\equiv1\pmod\ell$").

**7.2 `prop:rings:class-c`** (proposition, the class of a unit modulo $\ell$-power powers; P). Let $q$ be a prime power,
$\ell\nmid q$ a prime, $v=v_\ell(q-1)$, and $A=\F_q^\times/(\F_q^\times)^{\ell^v}$. (i) $A$ is cyclic of order $\ell^v$; the projection restricted
to the $\ell$-primary part $\mu_{\ell^v}(\F_q)$ of $\F_q^\times$ is an isomorphism onto $A$, and $u\mapsto u^{(q-1)/\ell^v}$ induces an isomorphism
$A\simeq\mu_{\ell^v}(\F_q)$. (ii) A generator $g$ of $\F_q^\times$ identifies $A$ with $\Z/\ell^v$: $u=g^a\mapsto c(u)=a\bmod\ell^v$. Changing $g$
multiplies $c(u)$ by a unit of $\Z/\ell^v$, so $v_\ell(c(u))$ (convention C3) does not depend on $g$. (iii) For $0\le k\le v$:
$u\in(\F_q^\times)^{\ell^k}$ iff $v_\ell(c(u))\ge k$; for $k\ge v$, $(\F_q^\times)^{\ell^k}=(\F_q^\times)^{\ell^v}$. (iv) For $n\in\Z\setminus\{0\}$:
$\min(v_\ell(n),v,v_\ell(c(u)))=\max\{k\le\min(v_\ell(n),v):u\in(\F_q^\times)^{\ell^k}\}$; it is $\ge1$ iff $\ell\mid n$, $q\equiv1\pmod\ell$ and $u$
is an $\ell$-th power in $\F_q^\times$. (v) For the paper's prime $q$ and a unit $u\in\Z_{(q)}^\times$ (or $u\in\Z_q^\times$, Chapter 7) the
class is that of the reduction $\bar u\in\F_q^\times$ (2.4(iii)). (The identification $A\simeq\F_q^\times\otimes\Z_\ell\simeq\Z_\ell(1)/(q-1)$ is
Chapters 3, 5, 7; the Kummer interpretation $u^{(q-1)/\ell^k}=\chi_u(\phi_q)$ is Chapter 5, item galois:kummer-finite-field.)
Reference: elementary. Paper: Lemma lem:tamfactor (statement: "$c\in\Z/\ell^{v_\ell(q-1)}$ the class of $u$ in
$\F_q^\times/(\F_q^\times)^{\ell^{v_\ell(q-1)}}$, so that $v_\ell(c)\ge k$ iff $u$ is an $\ell^k$-th power", and the "In particular" clause), Section
sec:tam, Remark rem:ko(1); Chapter 21, item A2.7.

**7.3 `ex:rings:powers`** (worked example; P). (a) $q=7$, $\ell=3$: $v=1$, the cubes are $\{1,6\}$; $\bar u=-1$ is a cube, $\bar u=2$ is
not. So a multiplicative prime $q=7$ with $3\mid n_7$ and unit part $u\equiv-1\pmod7$ contributes $v_3(n_7)+1$ to
$v_3(\Tam_7(\ad T))$ by Lemma lem:tamfactor. (b) $q=19$, $\ell=3$: $v=2$; $2$ is a primitive root mod $19$; the cubes are
$\{1,7,8,11,12,18\}$ and the ninth powers $\{1,18\}$; $c(2)=1$, $c(8)=3$, $c(-1)=c(2^9)=0$, so $v_3(c)=0,1,2$. (c) $q=13$, $\ell=3$:
$u$ is a cube iff $u^4=1$ (compare Chapter 5, Exercise 8). (d) $q=5$, $\ell=3$: every element is a cube; $q=11$, $\ell=5$: $v=1$,
the fifth powers are $\{\pm1\}$. Paper: Lemma lem:tamfactor, Section sec:tam.

### Section 8. Roots of unity and cyclotomic polynomials

**8.1 `def:rings:roots-of-unity`** (definition and proposition; P). $\mu_n(K)$, $\mu(K)=\bigcup_n\mu_n(K)$; a primitive $n$-th root of
unity is an element of order exactly $n$. (i) $\mu_n(K)$ is cyclic of order dividing $n$ (Chapter 1, item 4.2), of order $n$
iff $K$ contains a primitive $n$-th root of unity, which requires $\operatorname{char}K\nmid n$ (4.2). (ii) $\mu_n(\C)=\langle\zeta_n\rangle$;
its primitive elements are the $\zeta_n^a$, $\gcd(a,n)=1$, $\varphi(n)$ in number. (iii) $\mu(\Q)=\mu(\R)=\{\pm1\}$, $\mu(\Q(i))=\mu_4$,
$\mu(\Q(\zeta_3))=\mu_6$ (3.4(ii)). Reference: DF 13.6. Paper: background ($\zeta_3$, $i$, $\mu_{\ell^k}$, $\mu_3$ and $\mu_4$ in Lemma
lem:euler).

**8.2 `thm:rings:cyclotomic`** (theorem; P). Let $\Phi_n(x)=\prod_{1\le a\le n,\ \gcd(a,n)=1}(x-\zeta_n^a)\in\C[x]$. (i)
$x^n-1=\prod_{d\mid n}\Phi_d(x)$. (ii) $\Phi_n\in\Z[x]$, monic of degree $\varphi(n)$ (induction on $n$ and 4.1(i)). (iii)
$\Phi_n(x)=\prod_{d\mid n}(x^d-1)^{\mu(n/d)}$. (iv) $\Phi_p=x^{p-1}+\dots+x+1$; $\Phi_{p^k}(x)=\Phi_p(x^{p^{k-1}})$; $\Phi_{2m}(x)=\Phi_m(-x)$ for
odd $m>1$; $\Phi_3=x^2+x+1$, $\Phi_4=x^2+1$, $\Phi_6=x^2-x+1$, $\Phi_8=x^4+1$, $\Phi_{12}=x^4-x^2+1$. (v) $\Phi_n(1)=p$ if $n=p^k$ with
$k\ge1$, $\Phi_n(1)=1$ if $n$ has at least two distinct prime factors, $\Phi_1(1)=0$; $\Phi_n(0)=1$ for $n\ge2$. (vi) $\Phi_{p^k}$ is
irreducible over $\Q$ (Eisenstein at $p$ for $\Phi_{p^k}(x+1)$, whose reduction mod $p$ is $x^{\varphi(p^k)}$). The irreducibility of every
$\Phi_n$ and $\Gal(\Q(\zeta_n)/\Q)\simeq(\Z/n)^\times$ are Chapter 5 (item galois:cyclotomic). Reference: DF 13.6; Washington, Chapter 2.
Paper: background (Chapter 5; Chapter 6, item 5.1 uses (v), (vi)).

**8.3 `prop:rings:cyclotomic-mod-p`** (proposition; P). Let $p$ be a prime with $p\nmid n$. (i) $\Phi_n\bmod p$ divides $x^n-1$, so it is
separable over $\F_p$ (4.2), and its roots in any field of characteristic $p$ are exactly the elements of order $n$. (ii) For $a\in\Z$:
$p\mid\Phi_n(a)$ iff $a\bmod p$ has order exactly $n$ in $\F_p^\times$; hence every prime divisor $p\nmid n$ of $\Phi_n(a)$ satisfies
$p\equiv1\pmod n$. (iii) There are infinitely many primes $p\equiv1\pmod n$ (apply (ii) to $\Phi_n(Nn)$, $N$ large and divisible by
any given finite set of primes). (iv) $\Phi_n$ splits into linear factors mod $p$ iff $p\equiv1\pmod n$. The complete factorisation
pattern ($\varphi(n)/f$ factors of degree $f$, $f$ the order of $p$ mod $n$) is Chapter 5 (item galois:cyclotomic-mod-p). Reference:
Ireland-Rosen, Chapter 13 (or 7); Washington, Chapter 2. Paper: background (the primes $q\equiv1\pmod\ell$ of Lemma lem:tamfactor
are those with $\mu_\ell\subset\F_q$; Chapter 6, item 5.2).

### Section 9. Quadratic residues, the symbols of Legendre, Jacobi and Kronecker, reciprocity

**9.1 `def:rings:legendre`** (definition and proposition; P). For an odd prime $p$ and $a\in\Z$: $\bigl(\frac ap\bigr)=0$ if $p\mid a$, $1$ if
$a$ is a nonzero square mod $p$, $-1$ otherwise. (i) Euler's criterion: $\bigl(\frac ap\bigr)\equiv a^{(p-1)/2}\pmod p$ (7.1(iv)). (ii)
$\bigl(\frac{ab}p\bigr)=\bigl(\frac ap\bigr)\bigl(\frac bp\bigr)$; $a\mapsto\bigl(\frac ap\bigr)$ is the unique homomorphism $(\Z/p)^\times\to\{\pm1\}$ of order $2$; there are
$(p-1)/2$ residues and $(p-1)/2$ non-residues. (iii) $\bigl(\frac{-1}p\bigr)=(-1)^{(p-1)/2}$. (iv) For $p=3$: $\bigl(\frac a3\bigr)\equiv a\pmod3$ for all $a$.
Reference: Ireland-Rosen, Chapter 5; Serre, Course in Arithmetic, I.3. Paper: background; (iv) is the congruence
"$q\equiv\chi_{-3}(q)\pmod3$" of Proposition prop:forms (via 9.6, 10.5).

**9.2 `prop:rings:quadratic-gauss-sum`** (proposition; P, except the sign in (iii), R). Let $q$ be an odd prime, $K$ a field of
characteristic $\ne q$ containing a primitive $q$-th root of unity $\zeta$, and $g=\sum_{a=1}^{q-1}\bigl(\frac aq\bigr)\zeta^a\in K$. (i)
$g^2=q^*=(-1)^{(q-1)/2}q$ (as an element of $K$); in particular $g\ne0$, since $q\ne0$ in $K$. (ii) For $b$ prime to $q$, $\sum_a\bigl(\frac aq\bigr)\zeta^{ab}=\bigl(\frac bq\bigr)g$. (iii) Over $\C$ with
$\zeta=\zeta_q$: $g=\sqrt q$ if $q\equiv1\pmod4$ and $g=i\sqrt q$ if $q\equiv3\pmod4$ (Gauss; stated, not needed). (iv) Hence
$\sqrt{q^*}\in\Q(\zeta_q)$ (used by Chapter 5, item galois:sqrt-lstar (b)). Proof of (i): $g^2=\sum_{a,b}\bigl(\frac{ab}q\bigr)\zeta^{a+b}$, substitute
$b=ac$ and use $\sum_{a=1}^{q-1}\zeta^{a(1+c)}=q-1$ or $-1$ according as $c\equiv-1$ or not. Reference: Ireland-Rosen, Chapter 6;
Washington, Chapter 4. Paper: background (Theorem thm:main: $\Q(\sqrt{\ell^*})\subset\Q(\zeta_\ell)$, via Chapter 5).

**9.3 `thm:rings:qr`** (theorem, quadratic reciprocity; P). Let $p\ne q$ be odd primes. (i)
$\bigl(\frac pq\bigr)\bigl(\frac qp\bigr)=(-1)^{\frac{p-1}2\cdot\frac{q-1}2}$; equivalently $\bigl(\frac{q^*}p\bigr)=\bigl(\frac pq\bigr)$. (ii) (First supplement)
$\bigl(\frac{-1}p\bigr)=(-1)^{(p-1)/2}$. (iii) (Second supplement) $\bigl(\frac2p\bigr)=(-1)^{(p^2-1)/8}$, i.e. $1$ iff $p\equiv\pm1\pmod8$. Proof
of (i) by Gauss sums in characteristic $p$: let $f$ be the order of $p$ mod $q$, $\zeta\in\F_{p^f}$ a primitive $q$-th root of unity
(6.5(ii)), and $g$ as in 9.2 with $K=\F_{p^f}$; then $g^2=q^*$ and, by 1.6 and 9.2(ii), $g^p=\sum\bigl(\frac aq\bigr)\zeta^{ap}=\bigl(\frac pq\bigr)g$; since
$g\ne0$, $(q^*)^{(p-1)/2}=g^{p-1}=\bigl(\frac pq\bigr)$ in $\F_p$, and Euler's criterion gives $\bigl(\frac{q^*}p\bigr)=\bigl(\frac pq\bigr)$. Proof of (iii):
with $\zeta$ a primitive $8$-th root of unity in $\F_{p^2}$, $\tau=\zeta+\zeta^{-1}$ satisfies $\tau^2=2$ and $\tau^p=\zeta^p+\zeta^{-p}$, which is
$\tau$ if $p\equiv\pm1\pmod8$ and $-\tau$ if $p\equiv\pm3\pmod8$. Alternatives: Eisenstein's lemma (Exercise 13); the splitting of
$p$ in $\Q(\zeta_q)$ (Chapter 6, item 5.4). Checked for all odd primes below $400$. Reference: Ireland-Rosen, Chapters 5 and 6;
Serre, Course in Arithmetic, I.3. Paper: Lemma lem:twist (the character of $\Q(\sqrt{q^*})$ is "the quadratic character of
conductor $q$", 9.6), Theorem thm:main and the set $S_f$ ($\ell^*$), Chapters 5 (galois:quadratic-characters (c)) and 6 (4.2(iii)).

**9.4 `def:rings:jacobi`** (definition and proposition; P). For odd $n=\prod p_i^{e_i}>0$ and $a\in\Z$,
$\bigl(\frac an\bigr)=\prod\bigl(\frac a{p_i}\bigr)^{e_i}$. (i) It is multiplicative in $a$ and in $n$, depends only on $a\bmod n$, and is $0$ iff
$\gcd(a,n)>1$. (ii) For odd coprime $m,n>0$: $\bigl(\frac mn\bigr)\bigl(\frac nm\bigr)=(-1)^{\frac{m-1}2\cdot\frac{n-1}2}$; $\bigl(\frac{-1}n\bigr)=(-1)^{(n-1)/2}$;
$\bigl(\frac2n\bigr)=(-1)^{(n^2-1)/8}$ (from 9.3, because $n\mapsto(-1)^{(n-1)/2}$ and $n\mapsto(-1)^{(n^2-1)/8}$ are multiplicative on odd $n$). (iii)
Warning: $\bigl(\frac an\bigr)=1$ does not imply that $a$ is a square mod $n$: $\bigl(\frac2{15}\bigr)=1$ but $2$ is not a square mod $3$. (iv) The
algorithm computing $\bigl(\frac an\bigr)$ by reciprocity without factoring. Checked for odd $m,n<200$. Reference: Ireland-Rosen,
Chapter 5. Paper: background (computing Kronecker symbols; PARI's `kronecker`).

**9.5 `prop:rings:kronecker`** (definition and proposition; P for (i) to (iii), (v); S for (iv)). The Kronecker symbol
$\bigl(\frac an\bigr)$ for $a,n\in\Z$ (C4): for $n=\varepsilon2^e\prod p_i^{e_i}$, $\varepsilon=\pm1$, $\bigl(\frac an\bigr)=\bigl(\frac a\varepsilon\bigr)\bigl(\frac a2\bigr)^e\prod\bigl(\frac a{p_i}\bigr)^{e_i}$. Let
$D\ne0$ with $D\equiv0,1\pmod4$ (a *discriminant*). (i) $n\mapsto\bigl(\frac Dn\bigr)$ is completely multiplicative on $\Z\setminus\{0\}$
and vanishes exactly when $\gcd(D,n)>1$. (ii) For $n,n'>0$ with $n\equiv n'\pmod{|D|}$, $\bigl(\frac Dn\bigr)=\bigl(\frac D{n'}\bigr)$; and
$\bigl(\frac D{-n}\bigr)=\operatorname{sign}(D)\bigl(\frac Dn\bigr)$. Hence $\chi_D(a):=\bigl(\frac Da\bigr)$ (for $a\ne0$, and $\chi_D(0)=0$ unless $D=1$) is a Dirichlet
character modulo $|D|$ (Section 10), real (values in $\{0,\pm1\}$), with $\chi_D(-1)=\operatorname{sign}D$. Proof via Jacobi reciprocity 9.4(ii),
writing $D=2^sD'$ with $D'$ odd. (iii) Values at primes: $\chi_D(p)=\bigl(\frac Dp\bigr)$ (Legendre) for odd $p$; $\chi_D(2)=0$ if $4\mid D$,
$1$ if $D\equiv1\pmod8$, $-1$ if $D\equiv5\pmod8$. (iv) A *fundamental discriminant* is $D\equiv1\pmod4$ squarefree, or $D=4m$
with $m\equiv2,3\pmod4$ squarefree. Every fundamental discriminant $D\ne1$ is uniquely a product of pairwise coprime *prime
discriminants* $-4$, $8$, $-8$, $q^*$ ($q$ odd prime), and $\chi_D$ is the product of the corresponding characters; each of these is
primitive of conductor $4$, $8$, $8$, $q$, so $\chi_D$ is primitive of conductor $|D|$ (10.3(iv)). (Checked: for the $39$
fundamental discriminants with $|D|\le60$ the conductor of $\chi_D$ is $|D|$.) The identification of fundamental discriminants
with discriminants of quadratic fields, and $\chi_D(p)$ as the splitting law, are Chapter 6 (items 1.5, 4.1 to 4.3). (v)
$\chi_{DD'}=\chi_D\chi_{D'}$ on integers prime to $DD'$, and $\chi_{Df^2}(n)=\chi_D(n)$ for $\gcd(n,f)=1$ (so a non-fundamental
discriminant gives an imprimitive character). Checked: periodicity mod $|D|$ and the sign rule for all discriminants $|D|\le200$, $n<400$. Reference:
Washington, Chapter 3; Cohen, A Course in Computational Algebraic Number Theory, Section 1.4 (add to `bib/ch02.bib` if
cited). Paper: the table of Section 1 ($\chi_{-3}$, $\chi_{-4}$), Section 3 Example ($\chi_{-11}$, $\chi_{-7}$), Lemma lem:twist, Theorem
thm:twist ($\chi_S$); PLAN.md Section 6 row "$\chi_{-3},\chi_{-4},\chi_q,q^*,\ell^*$, Kronecker symbols".

**9.6 `prop:rings:qstar`** (proposition; P). Let $q$ be an odd prime and $q^*=(-1)^{(q-1)/2}q$. (i) $q^*\equiv1\pmod4$; $q^*$ is the
prime discriminant attached to $q$. (ii) For **every** integer $n$, $\bigl(\frac{q^*}n\bigr)=\bigl(\frac nq\bigr)$ (Legendre symbol of $n\bmod q$): at odd
primes $p\ne q$ by 9.3(i); at $n=2$ because $q^*\equiv1\pmod8$ iff $q\equiv\pm1\pmod8$ iff $\bigl(\frac2q\bigr)=1$ (9.3(iii)); at $n=-1$ because
$\operatorname{sign}(q^*)=(-1)^{(q-1)/2}=\bigl(\frac{-1}q\bigr)$; at $q$ both vanish. So $\chi_{q^*}=\bigl(\frac\cdot q\bigr)$ as functions on $\Z$. (iii) $\chi_{q^*}$ is the
unique Dirichlet character of order $2$ and conductor $q$ ($(\Z/q)^\times$ is cyclic, 5.3), which is why the paper calls it "the
quadratic character of conductor $q$" and writes $\chi_q$. By contrast, for $q\equiv3\pmod4$ the function $n\mapsto\bigl(\frac qn\bigr)$ is a
character of conductor $4q$ ($\bigl(\frac3\cdot\bigr)$ has conductor $12$). (iv) The same for the coefficient prime $\ell$: $\ell^*\equiv1\pmod4$, and
for primes $p\ne\ell$, $\ell^*$ is a square mod $p$ (for odd $p$), resp. $\ell^*\equiv1\pmod8$ (for $p=2$), iff $p$ is a square mod $\ell$. (v)
Table: $3^*=-3$, $5^*=5$, $7^*=-7$, $11^*=-11$, $13^*=13$; so in the paper's notation $\chi_3=\chi_{-3}$, $\chi_7=\chi_{-7}$,
$\chi_{11}=\chi_{-11}$. Checked: (ii) for all odd primes $q<200$ and all $|n|\le300$. Reference: Washington, Chapter 3;
Ireland-Rosen, Chapter 5. Paper: Section 1 ($q^*$, the twist $E'$ by $\Q(\sqrt{q^*})$), Section 1.4 and Lemma lem:twist ("$\chi=\chi_q$ the
quadratic character of conductor $q$ ... that is, by $\Q(\sqrt{q^*})$"), Theorem thm:main ($\ell^*$), Section 3 Example
($\chi_{-11}=\chi_{11}$, $\chi_{-7}=\chi_7$), Theorems thm:twist, thm:quant ($\chi_S$).

### Section 10. Dirichlet characters, conductors, orthogonality, Gauss sums; the paper's characters

**10.1 `def:rings:dirichlet`** (definition and proposition; P). A Dirichlet character mod $n$ (C5); the principal character
$\chi_0$ mod $n$; complete multiplicativity, $n$-periodicity, $\chi(a)$ a root of unity of order dividing the exponent of
$(\Z/n)^\times$ for $\gcd(a,n)=1$; $\bar\chi=\chi^{-1}$; parity: $\chi(-1)=\pm1$ (even/odd). The characters mod $n$ form a group $X(n)$
isomorphic (non-canonically) to $(\Z/n)^\times$ (via 5.2, 5.3), of order $\varphi(n)$. Characters with values in another field $k$
containing enough roots of unity are defined the same way; the example used later is the $\F_\ell^\times$-valued character
$\omega_\ell(a)=a\bmod\ell$ of conductor $\ell$ and order $\ell-1$, which is the Dirichlet character of the reduced cyclotomic
character $\bar\chi_\ell$ (Chapters 5, 6). Reference: Washington, Chapter 3; Serre, Course in Arithmetic, VI.1; Ireland-Rosen,
Chapter 16. Paper: Lemma lem:twist(iii), Theorems thm:twist and thm:quant ($a_n(g\otimes\chi)=\chi(n)a_n(g)$), Section 3 Example
($a_p(\text{121a1})\equiv\chi_{-11}(p)a_p(\text{11a1})\pmod3$).

**10.2 `thm:rings:orthogonality`** (theorem; P). For Dirichlet characters mod $n$: (i) $\sum_{a\bmod n}\chi(a)=\varphi(n)$ if $\chi=\chi_0$ and
$0$ otherwise; (ii) $\sum_{\chi\bmod n}\chi(a)=\varphi(n)$ if $a\equiv1\pmod n$ and $0$ otherwise; (iii) for $\gcd(a,n)=1$,
$\frac1{\varphi(n)}\sum_\chi\bar\chi(a)\chi(b)=1$ if $b\equiv a\pmod n$, else $0$; (iv) the characters mod $n$ form an orthonormal basis of the
functions on $(\Z/n)^\times$ for $\langle f,h\rangle=\varphi(n)^{-1}\sum f\bar h$ (RHB analogue: the character table of a finite abelian group;
finite Fourier analysis). Checked numerically for all characters mod $n<40$. Reference: Serre, Course in Arithmetic, VI.1;
Washington, Chapter 3. Paper: background (Chapter 19, Dirichlet $L$-functions; projections onto character eigenspaces).

**10.3 `prop:rings:conductor`** (definition and proposition; P). Let $\chi$ be a character mod $n$ and $d\mid n$. $\chi$ is *defined
mod $d$* (induced from a character $\chi'$ mod $d$) if $\chi(a)=\chi'(a)$ for all $a$ with $\gcd(a,n)=1$; equivalently $\chi(a)=1$ for all $a$
with $\gcd(a,n)=1$ and $a\equiv1\pmod d$. (i) If $\chi$ is defined mod $d_1$ and mod $d_2$ then it is defined mod $\gcd(d_1,d_2)$. (ii) Hence
there is a least such $d$, the *conductor* $f_\chi$; it divides every $d$ modulo which $\chi$ is defined; $\chi$ is induced by a unique
character $\chi^{\mathrm{prim}}$ mod $f_\chi$, which is *primitive* (its own conductor is $f_\chi$); $\chi$ is primitive iff $f_\chi=n$. (iii) $\chi$ and $\chi^{\mathrm{prim}}$
agree on integers prime to $n$, but may differ at $a$ with $\gcd(a,n)>1=\gcd(a,f_\chi)$: $\chi_{-3}$ regarded mod $6$ vanishes at $2$, while
$\chi_{-3}(2)=-1$. (iv) Writing $\chi=\prod_p\chi_p$ with $\chi_p$ a character mod $p^{v_p(n)}$ (5.2), $f_\chi=\prod_pf_{\chi_p}$; a product of primitive
characters with pairwise coprime conductors is primitive, with conductor the product. (v) Examples: the trivial character
has conductor $1$; the nontrivial character mod $4$ has conductor $4$ (not $2$); $\bigl(\frac\cdot q\bigr)$ has conductor $q$; $\chi_8$ ($=1$ iff
$n\equiv\pm1\pmod8$) and $\chi_{-8}$ ($=1$ iff $n\equiv1,3\pmod8$) have conductor $8$; $\chi_S=\prod_{q\in S}\chi_{q^*}$ ($S$ a finite set of odd
primes) is primitive of conductor $\prod_{q\in S}q$. Reference: Washington, Chapter 3; Ireland-Rosen, Chapter 16 (or
Miyake, Modular Forms, 3.1, which is in `refs.bib`). Paper: Lemma lem:twist ("the quadratic character of conductor $q$"),
Lemma lem:twist(iii) (Atkin-Li: the level of $g\otimes\chi$ involves the conductor of $\chi$), Theorems thm:twist and thm:quant
($\chi_S(n)=0$ when $\gcd(n,D)>1$, which is why "$\iota$ kills the coefficients at multiples of $D$"); Chapter 6, item 5.7.

**10.4 `thm:rings:gauss-sums`** (definition and theorem; P, except (iv), S). For a character $\chi$ mod $n$ and $b\in\Z$ put
$\tau(\chi,b)=\sum_{a\bmod n}\chi(a)\zeta_n^{ab}$ and $\tau(\chi)=\tau(\chi,1)$. (i) If $\gcd(b,n)=1$, $\tau(\chi,b)=\bar\chi(b)\tau(\chi)$. (ii) If $\chi$ is
primitive, $\tau(\chi,b)=\bar\chi(b)\tau(\chi)$ for **all** $b$ (both sides vanish when $\gcd(b,n)>1$), $|\tau(\chi)|^2=n$,
$\tau(\chi)\tau(\bar\chi)=\chi(-1)n$ and $\overline{\tau(\chi)}=\chi(-1)\tau(\bar\chi)$. (iii) $\tau\bigl(\bigl(\frac\cdot q\bigr)\bigr)^2=q^*$ (9.2);
$\tau(\chi_{-4})=i-(-i)=2i$; $\tau(\chi_{-3})=\zeta_3-\zeta_3^2=1+2\zeta_3=\sqrt{-3}$ (C6). (iv) For $\chi$ mod $n$ induced from primitive $\chi^{\mathrm{prim}}$ mod
$f$ with $\gcd(n/f,f)=1$: $\tau(\chi)=\mu(n/f)\chi^{\mathrm{prim}}(n/f)\tau(\chi^{\mathrm{prim}})$; e.g. $\chi_{-3}$ mod $6$ has $\tau=(-1)(-1)\sqrt{-3}=\sqrt{-3}$; $\tau(\chi_0)=\mu(n)$ (a
Ramanujan sum). Checked numerically: (i), (ii) for all $278$ primitive characters of modulus $3\le n<40$; (iii), (iv) as
stated. Reference: Washington, Chapter 4; Ireland-Rosen, Chapter 8. Paper: background (Chapter 5, galois:sqrt-lstar;
Chapter 19, root numbers and functional equations of twisted $L$-functions).

### Section 11. Valuations, the $p$-adic absolute value, and the paper's local terms

**11.1 `def:rings:valuation`** (definition and proposition; P). For a prime $p$, $v_p\colon\Q\to\Z\cup\{+\infty\}$, $v_p(0)=+\infty$,
$v_p(p^ka/b)=k$ for $a,b\in\Z$ prime to $p$ (C3). (i) $v_p(xy)=v_p(x)+v_p(y)$; (ii) $v_p(x+y)\ge\min(v_p(x),v_p(y))$, with equality if
$v_p(x)\ne v_p(y)$; (iii) $v_p(x)\ge0$ iff $x\in\Z_{(p)}$, $v_p(x)=0$ iff $x\in\Z_{(p)}^\times$ (2.4); (iv) on $\Z$: $p^k\mid n$ iff $v_p(n)\ge k$,
$v_p(\gcd)=\min$, $v_p(\operatorname{lcm})=\max$ (3.3); (v) $v_p(r\Z)=v_p(r)$ for ideals, and "the $p$-part" $p^{v_p(n)}$; (vi) Legendre's
formula $v_p(n!)=\sum_{k\ge1}\lfloor n/p^k\rfloor$ (exercise). Normalisation $v_\ell(\ell)=1$, as in Appendix B. Reference: Serre, Course in
Arithmetic, Chapter II; Koblitz, Chapter I. Paper: Definition def:terms, Theorem thm:main ($v_\ell(\deg\varphi_E)$), the
abstract, PLAN.md Section 6 row "$\ell$-adic valuations of integers and rationals".

**11.2 `def:rings:padic-abs`** (definition and proposition; P for (i) to (iii), R for (iv)). $|x|_p=p^{-v_p(x)}$, $|0|_p=0$; $|x|_\infty$
the usual absolute value. (i) $|\cdot|_p$ is an absolute value satisfying the ultrametric inequality $|x+y|_p\le\max(|x|_p,|y|_p)$;
$d_p(x,y)=|x-y|_p$ is a metric on $\Q$ in which $p^k\to0$. (ii) Product formula: $\prod_{p\le\infty}|x|_p=1$ for $x\in\Q^\times$. (iii) $\Q$
is not complete for $d_p$ (definition of the completion $\Q_p$ and of $\Z_p$: Chapter 7; $\Z_\ell$ as a profinite group:
Chapter 1, item 7.2; as a DVR: Chapter 3). (iv) Ostrowski: every nontrivial absolute value on $\Q$ is equivalent to $|\cdot|_\infty$
or to one $|\cdot|_p$ (stated). Reference: Koblitz, Chapter I; Neukirch, Chapter II. Paper: background (the field $\Q_q$ and the
ring $\Z_\ell$ throughout Sections 2 and 4).

**11.3 `lem:rings:ell-integral`** (lemma; P). Let $q\ne\ell$ be primes. (i) $v_\ell(q^k)=0$ for all $k\in\Z$: $q$ is an $\ell$-adic unit.
(ii) For $P\in\Z[X]$ of degree at most $d$ and $k\ge0$: $P(q^{-k})\in\Z_{(\ell)}$ and $v_\ell(P(q^{-k}))=v_\ell\bigl(q^{kd}P(q^{-k})\bigr)$, the
valuation of an integer; it is finite iff $P(q^{-k})\ne0$. (iii) Hence, for $\varepsilon=\pm1$ and $a\in\Z$:
$v_\ell\bigl(\tfrac{q-1}q\bigr)=v_\ell(q-1)$; $v_\ell(1-\varepsilon q^{-1})=v_\ell(q-\varepsilon)$; $v_\ell(1-q^{-2})=v_\ell(q^2-1)=v_\ell(q-1)+v_\ell(q+1)$ (5.4(v));
$v_\ell\bigl(\tfrac{q-1}q\cdot\tfrac{(q+1)^2-a^2}{q^2}\bigr)=v_\ell(q-1)+v_\ell\bigl((q+1)^2-a^2\bigr)$ when $(q+1)^2\ne a^2$. (iv) For $q=2$, $\ell=3$:
$v_3(1+2\cdot2^{-2})=v_3(3/2)=1$. All these valuations are $\ge0$. Reference: elementary. Paper: Definition def:terms
($e_\ell=v_\ell(P_q(q^{-2})/(1-a_q^2q^{-2}))$; at a multiplicative prime $a_q^2=1$ and the quotient is $1$; at an additive prime $a_q=0$),
the table of Section 1, Lemma lem:euler (proof: "$e_\ell=v_\ell(1-q^{-2})=v_\ell(q^2-1)$", "$e_\ell=v_\ell(1-\chi_{-3}(q)q^{-1})=v_\ell(q-\chi_{-3}(q))$"),
Lemma lem:fpr ("a nonzero rational number which is an $\ell$-adic integer"), Remark rem:wild ("$P_2(X)=1+2X$ and
$e_3=v_3(1+\frac12)=1$").

**11.4 `prop:rings:i0star-identity`** (proposition; P). Let $q>0$ and $a$ be real, and $\alpha,\beta\in\C$ the roots of $X^2-aX+q$. Then
$$\Bigl(1-\frac\alpha{\beta q}\Bigr)\Bigl(1-\frac1q\Bigr)\Bigl(1-\frac\beta{\alpha q}\Bigr)=\frac{q-1}q\cdot\frac{(q+1)^2-a^2}{q^2},$$
(expand, using $\alpha\beta=q$ and $\frac\alpha\beta+\frac\beta\alpha=\frac{a^2-2q}q$). Moreover $(q+1)^2-a^2=(q+1-a)(q+1+a)$, and if $|a|\le2\sqrt q$
(the Hasse bound, Chapter 9) then $(q+1)^2-a^2\ge(q-1)^2>0$, so for $q\ge2$ the valuation in 11.3(iii) is finite. Remark (forward
pointer): for an elliptic curve $E'/\F_q$ with $a=q+1-\#E'(\F_q)$, the factors are $\#E'(\F_q)$ and the order of the group of its
quadratic twist over $\F_q$ (Chapter 9). Checked symbolically. Reference: elementary. Paper: Lemma lem:euler (the displayed
identity for type $\mathrm I_0^*$), the table of Section 1, the abstract.

**11.5 `prop:rings:local-terms`** (proposition; P, given the table of Section 1 as stated in Chapters 19 and 21). Let $q\ge5$ be a
prime, $\ell\ne q$ a prime, and let $e$ be the value of $e_\ell(E,q)$ prescribed by the table. Then $e$ is a finite nonnegative
integer, and:
(i) types $\mathrm{II},\mathrm{II}^*,\mathrm{IV},\mathrm{IV}^*$: $e=v_\ell(q-\chi_{-3}(q))$, which is $v_\ell(q-1)$ if $q\equiv1\pmod3$ and $v_\ell(q+1)$ if $q\equiv2\pmod3$;
for $\ell=3$, $e\ge1$ always (10.5(i)), and $e$ is unbounded (e.g. $q=163$: $e=v_3(162)=4$); for $\ell\ge5$, $e\ge1$ iff $q\equiv\chi_{-3}(q)\pmod\ell$;
(ii) types $\mathrm{III},\mathrm{III}^*$: $e=v_\ell(q-\chi_{-4}(q))$; for $\ell=3$, $e\ge1$ iff $q\equiv\pm1\pmod{12}$;
(iii) type $\mathrm{I}_n^*$: $e=v_\ell(q^2-1)$; for $\ell=3$, $e\ge1$ always; for $\ell\ge5$, $e\ge1$ iff $q\equiv\pm1\pmod\ell$;
(iv) type $\mathrm{I}_0^*$, with $a=a_q(E')$: $e=v_\ell(q-1)+v_\ell((q+1)^2-a^2)$; for $\ell=3$, $e\ge1$ iff $q\equiv1\pmod3$ or $3\mid a$; for
$\ell\ge5$, $e\ge1$ iff $q\equiv1\pmod\ell$ or $a\equiv\pm(q+1)\pmod\ell$;
(v) type $\mathrm{I}_n$: $e=0$; the term $t_\ell(E,q)=v_\ell(-v_q(j_E))=v_\ell(n)$ and the Tamagawa refinement
$v_\ell(n)+\min(v_\ell(n),v_\ell(q-1),v_\ell(c))$ of Lemma lem:tamfactor exceeds $t_\ell$ iff $\ell\mid n$, $q\equiv1\pmod\ell$ and $\bar u\in(\F_q^\times)^\ell$ (7.2(iv)).
(Checked: (i) to (iii) for all primes $5\le q<10^5$; (iii) at $\ell=5,7,11,13$ for $q<2\cdot10^4$.) Reference: elementary given the
table. Paper: Definition def:terms, the table, Lemma lem:euler, Proposition prop:forms (proof: "$e_3(E,q)=v_3(q-\chi_{-3}(q))\ge1$
since $q\equiv\chi_{-3}(q)\pmod3$"), Remark after Theorem thm:quant, (1) ("a single prime of type $\mathrm I_n^*$ ... forces $3\mid\deg\varphi_E$,
and ... a prime of type $\mathrm I_0^*$ with $q\equiv1\pmod3$ does too"), Section sec:anomaly (residue classes mod $12$), Section
sec:iso, Section sec:tam.

### 4.2 Exercises (Section 12 of the chapter), graded from easy to harder

1. **Lemma lem:twist, proof of (ii): the invariants of the twist by $\Q(\sqrt{q^*})$.** Location: "the twist has a model with
   invariants $q^2c_4$, $q^3c_6$, $q^6\Delta$ and the same $j$-invariant". Problem: the quadratic twist by $d$ of
   $y^2=x^3+a_2x^2+a_4x+a_6$ is $y^2=x^3+da_2x^2+d^2a_4x+d^3a_6$, with invariants $d^2c_4$, $d^3c_6$, $d^6\Delta$ (checked symbolically). For the
   twist by $\Q(\sqrt{q^*})$, $d=q^*$, this gives $q^2c_4$, $(q^*)^3c_6=(-1)^{(q-1)/2}q^3c_6$, $q^6\Delta$. For $q\equiv3\pmod4$ the triple
   $(q^2c_4,q^3c_6,q^6\Delta)$ belongs to the twist by $q=-q^*$, that is, to $E^{(q)}\otimes\chi_{-4}$, which is also ramified at $2$. The next
   sentence of the proof writes the model with $q^*$ correctly. Fix: write "$(q^*)^2c_4=q^2c_4$, $(q^*)^3c_6$, $(q^*)^6\Delta=q^6\Delta$", or
   "invariants whose $q$-adic valuations are those of $c_4$, $c_6$, $\Delta$ increased by $2$, $3$, $6$". Effect: none, since only
   $q$-adic valuations are used there. Confidence: high.

2. **Introduction (paragraph "The classical route to the degree ..."): the description of the naive adjoint $L$-function.**
   Location: "The $L$-function that appears there is the *naive* one: the Euler product of $\sum a_n(f)^2n^{-s}$, which has the
   true local factor at the primes of good or multiplicative reduction and the trivial factor at the additive primes."
   Problem: at a good prime $p$, $\sum_ka_{p^k}^2p^{-ks}=(1+p^{1-s})L_p(\Sym f,s)$, not $L_p(\Sym f,s)$ (Rankin's identity; checked as an exact
   power series identity for several $(p,a_p)$). At multiplicative primes ($a_{p^k}^2=1$) and additive primes ($a_{p^k}=0$, $k\ge1$) the
   description is right. Globally $\sum a_n(f)^2n^{-s}=L^{\nv}(\Sym f,s)\,\zeta^{(N)}(s-1)/\zeta^{(N)}(2s-2)$, where $\zeta^{(N)}$ omits the
   Euler factors at $p\mid N$. Fix: "the naive one, $\prod_pL_p^{\nv}(\Sym f,s)$, which has the true local factor at the primes of good
   or multiplicative reduction and the trivial factor at the additive primes; it differs from $\sum a_n(f)^2n^{-s}$ by the factor
   $\zeta^{(N)}(s-1)/\zeta^{(N)}(2s-2)$". Effect: none on the results; Definition def:terms and Section 1.1 define the naive factor
   prime by prime, which is what is used. (This belongs to Chapter 19's area; it was found while checking Definition
   def:terms.) Confidence: high.
